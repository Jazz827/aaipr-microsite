Policy Dimension

Cross-Border Data Transfers

Why transfer rules matter, how African approaches compare, and why interoperability is the practical path.

Home/Cross-Border Data Transfers
1Definition

Moving data across a border, legally

A cross-border data transfer happens whenever personal data is sent, stored, or accessed outside the country it was collected in. Because personal data can reveal sensitive things about real people, most African countries only allow it to leave under specific legal conditions — not by default. This matters directly for infrastructure investment: transfer rules and localisation requirements shape where operators need to build capacity in the first place.

2Sovereignty Meets Trade

Why interoperability beats strict localisation

Data sovereignty and digital trade are often framed as opposites, but the more useful framing is a trade-off: strict, blanket localisation protects control but raises costs and can isolate a market from regional digital trade. Interoperability — designing transfer mechanisms so they can be mutually recognised across borders — is generally the more practical path, since it protects data while still letting regional digital trade function.

Regional and continental harmonisation efforts exist precisely to support this: they don't ask countries to give up control over their data, but to make that control compatible with their neighbours' rules.

3How Approaches Compare

Four markets, four approaches

The four countries profiled on this site illustrate the range well. Nigeria's Data Protection Act, 2023 permits transfer with legal safeguards, and pairs with a separate 40% local-data hosting mandate. Kenya's Data Protection Act, 2019 restricts transfer to countries with adequate safeguards under sections 48–49. South Africa's POPIA is unusually strict and specific, uniquely extending protection to companies as well as individuals. Ghana's 2012 Act leaves cross-border transfer largely unaddressed, creating regulatory uncertainty rather than regulatory strictness. See full detail in each Country Profile.

4Continental Alignment

Two frameworks, uneven adoption

Two African Union instruments sit above national laws. The Malabo Convention (adopted 2014) is the AU's binding treaty on cybersecurity and data protection — it entered into force in June 2023 after reaching 15 ratifications, calling for national data protection authorities and cross-border cooperation. The AU Data Policy Framework (endorsed 2022) is a non-binding blueprint for an African Digital Single Market, aimed at enabling data to flow across the continent while safeguarding rights.

In practice, alignment remains uneven — some major economies had not ratified the Malabo Convention even after it entered into force, and national laws differ sharply in detail and strictness. For a business planning infrastructure across more than one African market, this means there's no single continental standard to design for yet — only a shared direction of travel.

Concluding Takeaway Cross-border data transfer in Africa is allowed almost everywhere, but rarely automatic anywhere — and the rules directly shape where infrastructure investment makes sense. See Recommendations for a realistic, phased path toward closing the gap.
