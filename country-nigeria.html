Nigeria

West Africa's largest digital economy, with a fast-expanding colocation market and a hard local-data mandate.

1Market Maturity

Where the market stands today

Nigeria is one of the fastest-growing data centre markets on the continent, anchored by Lagos as the commercial hub. Africa Data Centres and MainOne (now part of Equinix) both operate significant colocation capacity, drawing on Nigeria's position as West Africa's largest economy and internet population.

2Infrastructure Readiness

Power, fibre, and physical constraints

Grid reliability outside South Africa is a genuine constraint, and Nigeria is a clear example: operators often budget diesel generation for extended, weeks-long gaps rather than brief outages, pushing energy costs to 55-65% of operating expense — well above the 35-45% typical in mature markets. Fibre and submarine cable landing infrastructure around Lagos, however, is comparatively strong.

3Policy & Regulatory Context

The legal environment

Governed by the Data Protection Act, 2023 and the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC), which permits cross-border transfer with legal safeguards. Nigeria also enforces a government IT mandate requiring a minimum share — around 40% — of certain data to be hosted locally, a factor directly accelerating migration from on-premise server rooms to carrier-neutral facilities.

4Demand Drivers

What's fuelling growth

A large, young, mobile-first population; a fast-growing fintech and e-commerce sector; and the local-hosting mandate itself, which structurally pushes demand toward domestic colocation capacity.

5Connectivity

Fibre and network access

Lagos benefits from multiple submarine cable landings, making it one of the best-connected cities in West Africa — though inland fibre reach beyond the coast remains comparatively limited.

6Investment Relevance

What this means for investors and developers

High growth potential paired with real operating-cost pressure from power unreliability. Projects that budget realistically for backup generation and diesel costs — rather than assuming grid-standard uptime — tend to perform far better than those that don't.
