Physical Dimension

Data Infrastructure

The physical layer of data centres, networks, and constraints that decides whether data can actually move — and where it can stay.

Home/Data Infrastructure
1Overview

The layer beneath the law

Every cross-border data transfer ultimately depends on physical infrastructure: somewhere for the data to be stored, a network to move it, and power to keep it all running. Africa's data-infrastructure footprint is growing fast, but it remains concentrated in a handful of hubs — and constrained by real physical, financial, and environmental limits.

2The Building Blocks

Data centres, cloud, fibre, and IXPs

Data centres

A data centre is a physical facility built to store, process, and secure digital information — the buildings behind cloud platforms, banking systems, government services, and AI tools. Capacity is expanding across the continent, but remains concentrated in a few hubs.

Cloud services

Most organisations rent capacity from cloud services rather than running their own hardware. Choosing a cloud provider often decides which country your data physically sits in.

Fibre networks

Cabling — including submarine cables — carries data at high speed between cities, data centres, and continents. Weak inland fibre coverage remains a real bottleneck in several markets.

Internet exchange points

An internet exchange point (IXP) lets local networks connect directly to each other, so local data doesn't have to take an expensive detour through an international route.

3Constraints, In Detail

Eight things that limit what can be built

Tap each constraint to expand it.

01Electricity
Data centres are power-hungry by design, and a stable power supply is one of the biggest factors determining where new capacity gets built. Unreliable grids mean expensive backup generation, higher operating costs, and lower resilience.
02Cooling
Servers generate constant heat that has to go somewhere. Cooling constraints — the difficulty and expense of keeping equipment at a safe operating temperature in hot climates — directly limit how large a facility can grow and how much it costs to run. See Environmental Recommendations for sustainable cooling strategies.
03Water
Many cooling systems rely on water, which competes with agricultural and municipal needs in water-stressed regions. Water availability is increasingly treated as a site-selection factor, not an afterthought.
04Connectivity
Carrier-neutral facilities and strong local interconnection through IXPs reduce reliance on costly international routes and improve both speed and resilience — connectivity quality is really the same question as "how close is the nearest data centre."
05Cost
Construction and equipment are often imported and priced in foreign currency, making African data-centre builds disproportionately expensive relative to local costs of capital and operating margins.
06Land & zoning
Suitable land needs the right zoning, proximity to power and fibre, and clear title — a slower and more uncertain process in many markets than the technical build itself.
07Technical skills
Specialised roles — data-centre engineers, network operators, cooling technicians — are in short supply in several markets, slowing both construction and ongoing operations.
08Environmental exposure
Flood risk, extreme heat, and climate volatility all affect where a facility can safely and sustainably operate over its lifetime. See Environmental Recommendations for full site-selection criteria.

These constraints, taken together, help explain why much African data is still hosted outside the continent even as demand for local infrastructure keeps growing.
