Concept 02

Data Infrastructure

The physical layer of data centres, networks, and power that decides whether data can actually move — and where it can stay.

Home/Data Infrastructure
1Overview

The layer beneath the law

Every cross-border data transfer ultimately depends on physical infrastructure: somewhere for the data to be stored, a network to move it, and power to keep it all running. Africa's data-infrastructure footprint is growing fast, but it's still concentrated in a handful of hubs — and still constrained by real physical limits.

2Data Centres

Where data actually lives

A data centre is a physical facility built to store, process, and secure digital information — the buildings behind cloud services, online banking, government systems, mobile apps, and AI tools. Data-centre capacity is expanding across the continent, but growth remains concentrated in a few hubs, with Nigeria and Kenya among the leading markets.

Cloud services

Most organisations don't run their own data centres — they rent capacity from cloud services, which package storage and computing power as an on-demand service. This is often what actually triggers a cross-border transfer: choosing a cloud provider can mean choosing which country your data physically sits in.

3Connectivity

The networks that carry the data

Data centres don't work alone — they depend on fibre networks and submarine cables to move data efficiently between cities and continents, and on internet exchange points (IXPs) to interconnect local networks without routing everything through an expensive international link.

Carrier-neutral facilities and strong local interconnection reduce reliance on costly international routes and improve both speed and resilience — which is a big part of why "where is the nearest data centre" and "how good is local connectivity" are really the same question.

4Power & Cooling

The constraints that shape everything else

Data centres are power-hungry by design, and a stable power supply is one of the biggest factors determining where new capacity gets built. Running short of it means backup generation, higher operating costs, and less resilience.

Servers also generate heat that has to go somewhere. Cooling constraints — the difficulty and expense of keeping equipment at a safe operating temperature in hot climates — directly affect how big a facility can get and how much it costs to run.

Main constraints on African data infrastructure

  • Unreliable electricity
  • High cooling demands
  • Expensive construction and imported equipment
  • Weak inland fibre coverage in some areas
  • Shortages of specialised technical skills

These challenges help explain why much African data is still hosted outside the continent, even as demand for local infrastructure keeps growing.
